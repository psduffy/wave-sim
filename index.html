The physics behind each layer, with sources

1. Wind to waves: fetch- and depth-limited growth

The wind stress factor UA = 0.71 U101.23 feeds the Sverdrup–Munk–Bretschneider shallow-water growth curves (Shore Protection Manual 1984, eqs. 3-39 and 3-40). The tanh factors in d cap the growth: a shallow bed drains energy from the long waves through bottom friction and percolation, so the same wind over Lake Erie makes a lower, shorter, steeper sea than over Lake Superior.

gH/UA² = 0.283 · tanh[0.530 (gd/UA²)3/4] · tanh{ 0.00565 (gF/UA²)1/2 / tanh[0.530 (gd/UA²)3/4] }
gT/UA = 7.54 · tanh[0.833 (gd/UA²)3/8] · tanh{ 0.0379 (gF/UA²)1/3 / tanh[0.833 (gd/UA²)3/8] }

The generation depth d is the mean depth between the upwind shore and the point. A wind that has not blown long enough is duration-limited: the fetch it can "fill" in time t follows gt/UA = 68.8 (gF/UA²)2/3 (SPM eq. 3-35), and the sea here grows on the lake clock.

The wind slider runs to 450 kn (232 m/s), about twice the strongest gust ever measured on Earth. The curves were fitted to storms below about 40 m/s; beyond that the simulator extrapolates them for fun. Two things are made safe for it: the drag coefficient is capped at 2.5 × 10⁻³, where it levels off in real hurricanes (Powell, Vickery and Reinhold 2003), and the lake model lets the upwind bed go dry rather than run out of water, so a hurricane over Erie blows the western basin empty and stacks the lake against Buffalo.

2. The spectrum

Hs and Tp are spread over 72 log-spaced frequency components with a JONSWAP shape (γ = 3.3) multiplied by the TMA depth factor of Bouws et al. (1985), which removes long-wave energy the depth cannot support. Each component gets a fixed random phase, so the surface is a realistic irregular sea rather than a single sine wave.

3. Propagation over the bed: linear wave theory

Each component keeps its frequency and solves the dispersion relation for the local wavenumber. In deep water (d > L/2) the bed is invisible; in shallow water (d < L/20) every wave travels at √(gd).

ω² = g k tanh(kd)   C = ω/k   Cg = ½ (1 + 2kd / sinh 2kd) · C

Energy flux E·Cg is conserved along the section, and E ∝ H², which gives the shoaling coefficient Ks = √(Cg,gen / Cg,local). Ks first dips to 0.91 (around d/L0 = 0.16, where Cg peaks) and then climbs steeply toward the shore.

4. Breaking

Where Hs would exceed 0.6 d (individual waves break near H = 0.78 d, McCowan 1894) or the Miche steepness limit, the surplus energy is removed and stays removed; the wind rebuilds the sea downwind over an e-folding fetch of 20 km. Foam marks the breaking zone.

5. Crest shape

Real waves have sharper crests and flatter troughs than a sine. The second-order Stokes term k a² · cosh kd (2 + cosh 2kd) / (4 sinh³ kd) · cos 2θ is added per component, capped where the Ursell number H L²/d³ makes Stokes theory meaningless (shallow water, where waves become cnoidal).

6. Orbits

Water particles move in closed loops: circles that shrink exponentially with depth in deep water, ellipses that flatten toward the bed in shallow water, where the horizontal motion reaches the bottom. Semi-axes A = a cosh k(z+d)/sinh kd and B = a sinh k(z+d)/sinh kd. This is why shallow Lake Erie stirs up its own mud in a storm.

7. The vessel

The ship is drawn at true length, draft and freeboard (loaded condition) and floats hydrostatically: it sits at the mean level and mean slope of the water along its hull, found by a least-squares line through the surface under it. No inertia, added mass or damping, so it is exact for a hull much shorter or much longer than the waves and a fair sketch in between. A 150 m tanker on 60 m waves spans more than two crests and rides nearly level; the dangerous sea is one whose wavelength approaches the hull length, when the ship pitches hardest and is alternately hogged (supported amidships) and sagged (supported at the ends). Where the water is shallower than the draft the keel is drawn on the bed. The window scale applies to the ship too, so with vertical exaggeration the hull looks deeper than it is.

8. Set-up and seiche: the lake as a whole

The wind stress τ = ρair CD U10² (CD from Wu 1982) tilts the surface until the pressure gradient balances it, with slope dη/dx = τ / (ρ g h): three times steeper over the 8 m western basin of Erie than over the 24 m central basin, and almost flat over Superior. The model integrates the one-dimensional shallow-water equations on a staggered grid with quadratic bottom friction:

∂η/∂t = −(1/b) ∂(b h u)/∂x    ∂u/∂t = −g ∂η/∂x + τ/(ρ h) − Cf |u| u / h

Change the wind and the tilted surface overshoots and rings as a seiche whose period the model produces on its own. The estimate shown is T1 = 2 ∫ dx / √(g h), which reduces to Merian's 2L/√(gh) for a uniform basin; Erie's observed fundamental is about 14.3 h.

Limits of a 1-D section

  • No refraction, no directional spreading, no islands: the real wave field is two-dimensional.
  • Plan-view width enters only the continuity equation. The funnelling that concentrates storm surge at Buffalo is a 2-D effect this model cannot reproduce.
  • The SMB curves are empirical; the Coastal Engineering Manual (2002) prefers friction-velocity scaling for deep water. The depth-limited form used here remains the standard quick method for lakes.
  • Profiles are rounded from NOAA Great Lakes bathymetry along the long axis, so they run deeper than each lake's area-mean depth.
